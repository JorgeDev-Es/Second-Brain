# /b/
The IPA sound /b/ is known as the **voiced bilabial plosive**. Let's break down what each of those terms means:

- **Voiced:** This means that your vocal cords vibrate when you produce the sound. You can feel this by placing your hand on your throat while saying "buh." You should feel a buzzing sensation.
    
- **Bilabial:** This refers to the place of articulation, meaning where in your mouth the sound is made. "Bilabial" means "two lips," so you bring both your upper and lower lips together to form the obstruction for this sound.
    
- **Plosive (or Stop):** This describes the manner of articulation, or how the air is released. For a plosive, the airflow is completely stopped in the vocal tract (by your lips, in this case), and then released suddenly in a small burst of air.
    

So, to produce /b/, you close your lips completely, build up a bit of air pressure behind them, turn on your vocal cords (making a buzzing sound in your throat), and then release your lips suddenly, letting the air burst out.
## German
The use of the /b/ sound in German is largely governed by its **position** within a word or syllable, particularly due to a phenomenon called **final devoicing (Auslautverhärtung)**.

- **When /b/ is pronounced as a voiced bilabial plosive:**
    - At the **beginning of a word**: For example, **B**all (ball) or **B**rot (bread).
    - In the **middle of a word or syllable**, when it's not at the very end of that syllable: For instance, lie**b**en (to love) or A**b**end (evening). In these positions, the /b/ maintains its full voiced, plosive quality.
- **When /b/ changes its pronunciation (final devoicing):**
    - When the "b" letter appears at the **end of a word** or the **end of a syllable**, it undergoes **devoicing**. This means it's pronounced as its voiceless counterpart, **/p/** (the voiceless bilabial plosive). For example, **ob** (if) is pronounced as /ɔp/ and **gelb** (yellow) as /ɡɛlp/. This is a crucial rule for correct German pronunciation.
