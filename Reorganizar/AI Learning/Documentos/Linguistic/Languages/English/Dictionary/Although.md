# Although

The word **although** is primarily used as a conjunction meaning "despite the fact that" or "even if," introducing a subordinate clause that presents a concession or contrast.

## Meaning

### Despite the fact that...

This usage of **although** indicates "in spite of the fact that" or "even though." It introduces a clause that expresses a fact or condition that makes the main clause seem surprising or unexpected. It's used as a conjunction.

#### Examples

> "Although it was raining, we went for a walk." This means "Despite the fact that it was raining, we went for a walk." "She decided to go out, although she felt tired." This means "She decided to go out, despite the fact that she felt tired."

### Even if...

In some contexts, **although** can carry a meaning closer to "even if" or "in case," implying a hypothetical concession. This usage is less common than "despite the fact that." It's used as a conjunction.

#### Examples

> "I'll try to help, although I'm not sure if I can." This means "I'll try to help, even if I'm not sure if I can." "Although he may seem quiet, he's actually very outspoken." This means "Even if he may seem quiet, he's actually very outspoken."

## Phonetic and pronunciation

The **strong form** of **although** is typically used when the word is emphasized, at the beginning of a sentence or a clause where it carries significant weight, or when pronounced carefully.

- **Pronunciation:** /ɔːlˈðoʊ/ (American English) or /ɔːlˈðəʊ/ (British English)
- **Sounds:**
    - The "al" sounds like "all" or "awl."
    - The "th" sound is voiced, like in "this" or "them."
    - The "ough" sounds like the "o" in "go" or "snow."

The **weak form** of **although** is very common in everyday, connected speech, especially when it is not being emphasized. The initial vowel sound may be slightly reduced, and the word blends more smoothly with surrounding words.

- **Pronunciation:** /əlˈðoʊ/ (American English) or /əlˈðəʊ/ (British English) – the initial /ɔː/ sound is often reduced to a schwa /ə/ or a less prominent vowel.
- **Sounds:** The initial "al" sound is quicker and less fully pronounced. The "th" and "ough" sounds remain similar to the strong form but are articulated more quickly and with less individual emphasis.

## Synonyms

When "although" means "despite the fact that" or "even if," it introduces a contrasting idea.

- **Though:** This is a very common synonym and is often interchangeable with "although." "Though" is sometimes considered slightly less formal.
    - _Example:_ **Though** it was raining, we went for a walk.
- **Even though:** This is a stronger, more emphatic version of "although" or "though," highlighting a greater degree of contrast.
    - _Example:_ **Even though** she was exhausted, she finished the marathon.
- **While:** Can sometimes be used to introduce a contrasting idea, especially when implying "whereas" or "despite the fact that."
    - _Example:_ **While** I understand your concerns, I think we should proceed.
- **Albeit:** (More formal) Meaning "although it be" or "even if it be." It is often used to introduce a concession about a previous statement.
    - _Example:_ He finally agreed, **albeit** reluctantly.
- **Notwithstanding (that):** (More formal) Meaning "in spite of the fact that."
    - _Example:_ **Notwithstanding that** he was ill, he attended the meeting.
- **In spite of the fact that:** A more wordy but explicit synonym.
    - _Example:_ **In spite of the fact that** it was late, they continued working.
- **Despite the fact that:** Similar to "in spite of the fact that."
    - _Example:_ **Despite the fact that** she was nervous, she gave a brilliant presentation.
