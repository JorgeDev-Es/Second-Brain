# T
The German "t" is typically pronounced as a **voiceless alveolar plosive**.

- **IPA Symbol:** **/t/**
    
- **How it's produced:**
    
    - **Voiceless:** Your vocal cords do not vibrate when producing this sound.
    - **Alveolar:** The tip or blade of your tongue touches the **alveolar ridge** (the bumpy ridge behind your upper front teeth).
    - **Plosive (or Stop):** The airflow is completely stopped by the tongue, and then released suddenly in a burst of air.
- **Comparison to English "t":** This is where it gets interesting, as there's a key difference related to **aspiration**, similar to what we discussed with /p/ vs. /pʰ/.
    
    - **German "t":** The German /t/ is generally **unaspirated**. This means there is little to no "puff of air" released after the sound. It's a clean, sharp release without the added breathiness.
        
        - Example: **Tag** (day) /taːk/
        - Example: **tun** (to do) /tuːn/
        - Example: **Gott** (God) /ɡɔt/
    - **English "t":** In English, the /t/ is often **aspirated** (th) when it occurs at the beginning of a stressed syllable (e.g., "**t**op," "**t**ime"). If you put your hand in front of your mouth and say "top" in English, you'll feel a clear puff of air. This aspiration is less pronounced or absent when "t" follows "s" (e.g., "stop") or at the end of a word (e.g., "cat").
        
    - **Implication for learners:** For English speakers, not aspirating the German "t" (and "p" and "k") is crucial for sounding more native. If you aspirate it like in English, it might sound a bit harsh or simply not quite right.
        
- **Comparison to Spanish "t":** The German /t/ is quite similar to the **Spanish "t"** in terms of being unaspirated. Both languages typically produce a "t" without the strong puff of air found in English. However, the exact place of articulation can sometimes differ slightly. The Spanish "t" is often described as slightly more "dental" (tongue touching the teeth or very close to them) compared to the German "t" which is more consistently alveolar. But the lack of aspiration is the main shared feature.
    

---

### Other Important Considerations for German "t":

- **Final Devoicing:** While "t" is already voiceless, it's worth noting that its occurrence at the end of a word means it won't be affected by final devoicing (as "d" would be, changing from /d/ to /t/ at the end of a word).
- **"tsch" and "z":**
    - The "tsch" combination represents the **affricate** sound **/t͡ʃ/** (like "ch" in English "**ch**urch").
    - The letter "z" represents the **affricate** sound **/t͡s/** (like "ts" in English "ca**ts**"). These are not just a "t" sound, but a combination of a stop and a fricative.
