# R sound
### The Consonantal "R" (at the beginning or middle of a word/syllable)

When the letter "r" appears at the **beginning of a word or a syllable**, or **before a vowel** within a word, it is pronounced as a **consonantal sound**. This "r" is very different from what you're used to in Spanish or English.

- **IPA Symbol:** The most common pronunciation in Standard German is a **voiced uvular fricative** [ʁ] or a **uvular trill** [ʀ]. While the trill is more common in some regions (like parts of Southern Germany, Austria, and Switzerland), the fricative is generally considered the standard.
    
- **How it's produced:** This sound is made in the **back of your throat**, where the back of your tongue (the uvula) either vibrates (trill) or comes very close to the uvula, creating friction as air passes through (fricative). It's often described as a "gargling" sound or a "raspy" sound. It's similar in place of articulation to the "ch" sound in German words like "Bach" or "Loch," but voiced (with vocal cord vibration).
    
- **Differences from Spanish and English "r"s:**
    
    - **Spanish "r":** Spanish "r" sounds ([r] and [ɾ]) are made with the **tip of the tongue** touching the alveolar ridge (behind your front teeth). The German consonantal "r" is made much further back in the throat.
    - **English "r":** The English "r" ([ɹ]) is typically made by curling the **tip of the tongue** back towards the roof of the mouth (retroflex) or by raising the tongue body near the alveolar ridge without full contact (approximant). Again, the German consonantal "r" is articulated much further back.
- **Examples:**
    
    - **R**ot (red) [ʁoːt]
    - **R**adio (radio) [ˈʁaːdi̯o]
    - Fah**r**en (to drive) [ˈfaːʁən]
    - G**r**ün (green) [ɡʁyːn]

### The Vocalic "R" (at the end of a word or syllable)

When the letter "r" appears at the **end of a word or a syllable**, especially after a vowel, it often undergoes **vocalization**. This means it's no longer pronounced as a distinct consonant, but rather as a **vowel-like sound**.

- **IPA Symbol:** This sound is usually transcribed as a **near-open central vowel** [ɐ] (a rotated lowercase "a"). It's similar to the unstressed "er" sound in some non-rhotic English accents (like some British English accents, where "car" might sound like "cah").
- **How it's produced:** Instead of articulating a consonant in the back of the throat, the "r" essentially merges with the preceding vowel, often lengthening it slightly or creating a diphthong-like quality. It's a very relaxed, almost "swallowed" sound.
- **Examples:**
    - De**r** (the, masc. nom.) [deːɐ̯]
    - Me**er** (sea) [meːɐ̯]
    - Mutte**r** (mother) [ˈmʊtɐ]
    - Geste**r**n (yesterday) [ˈɡɛstɐn]
    - **Ver**lieren (to lose) → The "r" in the prefix "ver-" is vocalized because it's at the end of the syllable: [fɛɐ̯ˈliːʁən] (notice the second "r" is consonantal because it's at the beginning of a syllable).
