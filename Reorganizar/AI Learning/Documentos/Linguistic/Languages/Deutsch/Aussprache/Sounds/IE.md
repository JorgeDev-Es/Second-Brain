# 'ie'
The German "ie" spelling typically represents a **long "i" vowel sound**.

- **IPA Symbol:** The sound is usually transcribed as the **long close front unrounded vowel** **/iː/**.
    
- **How it's produced:**
    
    - **Close:** Your tongue is held high in your mouth, very close to the roof of your mouth.
    - **Front:** The highest point of your tongue is towards the front of your mouth, behind your teeth.
    - **Unrounded:** Your lips are spread or neutral, not rounded.
    - **Long:** This is the crucial part. The sound is held for a longer duration compared to a short vowel. This length is phonemically important in German, meaning it can distinguish word meanings.
- **Comparison to English:**
    
    - This German "ie" sound is very similar to the vowel sound in English words like **"see," "me," "tree,"** or the "ee" in **"feel."** It's the "long E" sound in English.
    - The key is that in English, this sound is often slightly diphthongized (meaning it has a slight glide at the end, like "ee-yuh"), whereas in German, it's typically a **pure monophthong** (a single, stable vowel sound).
- **Examples:**
    
    - **Liebe** (love) → /'liːbə/
    - **spielen** (to play) → /'ʃpiːlən/
    - **vier** (four) → /fiːɐ̯/ (Note: the "r" here is vocalized to /ɐ/ as we discussed)
    - **diese** (these) → /'diːzə/
    - **Sie** (you - formal) → /ziː/
- **Contrast with "i" (short i):** It's important to distinguish "ie" (/iː/) from the short "i" sound, usually spelled with a single "i" in German (unless it's followed by a double consonant or a specific consonant cluster).
    
    - The **short "i"** is the **near-close near-front unrounded vowel** **/ɪ/**.
    - It's like the vowel sound in English words such as **"sit," "kit," "pin."**
    - **Example:** **Sinn** (sense) /zɪn/ vs. **Sieg** (victory) /ziːk/ (Notice the final devoicing of "g" to "k" in "Sieg").
